Food Deserts – Grocery Store Design Challenge

In some neighborhoods, the nearest supermarket is more than a mile away, and many families don't have a car. Where would you build a new store, and what would it look like?

  1. Explore food deserts

    Look at real U.S. neighborhoods. Find who lives far from a supermarket, where the stores and bus routes are, and where there's land to build on.

  2. Place your store

    Move a 3D supermarket around the map and see how many people could be served by your store. Find the best spot and fit the store to the land.

  3. Explain it and design it

    Explain why you chose your spot and download your site plan. Then use the plan to design and build your store in SketchUp.

About 45 minutes · no sign-in · real data from the U.S. Census Bureau, USDA and U.S. Department of Transportation

Food Deserts LABGrocery Store Design Challenge

Some neighborhoods have no supermarket nearby. They are called food deserts. Your job: find the best place to build a new supermarket, then design it in SketchUp.

1Step 1: Explore the food deserts about 10 min

A neighborhood is a food desert when both are true:

1At least 20% of people live in poverty.
2The nearest supermarket is more than 1 mile away.

Your job: look at each food desert. Press Details, or click a red area on the map. Choose the one you want to help.

    2Step 2: Place your store about 15–20 min

    The store follows the mouse. Watch the numbers in the box on the map change. Click to put it down. Look for empty land or parking lots, not houses.

    Fit it to the site

    Drag on the map, or use the controls below. Press Done on the map when you have finished.

    • Drag the store to move it.
    • Drag a white dot to make that side longer or shorter.
    • ⟳Drag the orange circle to turn it to line up with the roads.

    Controls (they work with a keyboard too)

    Move 10 ft
    Extra challenge: is there enough land? (optional)

    3Step 3: Explain your choice about 10 min

    Explain your thinking in as much detail as you need. Use numbers from the box on the map. You could start with:

    • We chose this location because…
    • A store here would serve ___ people, including ___ households without a car.
    • This site is better than other places we tried because…
    • One problem with this site is…

    4Step 4: Create your site plan about 5 min

    The map view becomes the picture in your document. Move the map yourself, or press Show my store.

    Your site plan has your picture, numbers, reasons and the store size, ready for building it in SketchUp. It is made on this device. Nothing is sent anywhere.

    Change the rules (teachers)

    USDA counts a census tract as low income if its poverty rate is 20% or more, or its median family income is 80% or less of the area median. This app uses the poverty part only. USDA's usual low-access distance for cities is 1 mile. The distance rule is also how far your store reaches.

    About the data and method

    Neighborhoods: census tracts from the U.S. Census Bureau American Community Survey 5-year estimates, via ArcGIS Living Atlas (tables B17020 poverty and B08201 vehicles available). Estimates have margins of error.

    People counts use the census population whose poverty status is known. This leaves out people living in group quarters such as college dorms and nursing homes, so totals are a little low.

    Food stores: USDA SNAP authorized retailers. “Supermarket” means SNAP store type Supermarket or Super Store.

    Transit: U.S. DOT Bureau of Transportation Statistics National Transit Map. 3D buildings: Esri 3D Buildings, ArcGIS Living Atlas.

    Food desert is the everyday name. USDA now calls these areas low-income and low-access (LILA) census tracts.

    Food desert areas are groups of touching food desert neighborhoods.

    People served assumes people are spread evenly across each neighborhood. A person counts if they live within the distance rule (1 mile) of your store and are currently more than that distance from any supermarket. Distances are straight-line, not walking routes.

    Minimum plot size is a rule of thumb: 3 × the store floor area, to leave room for parking and deliveries.

    USDA’s official Food Access Research Atlas uses finer grids and more income measures. Results here are for learning, not official designations.
